The advisor was designed around three people we interviewed. They are not
three made-up customers: they are three members of one family, and their
answers were recorded before the advisor was built. What separates them is
not age or job. It is the state of their insurance, because that is what
changes the answer they need.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROFILE 1 — DAD: the settled user
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dad has been on the same insurance plan for years. Nothing about his coverage
has changed, and when the family needed a specialist he already knew where to
go. Nobody has ever asked him which hospital takes his insurance, because it
has never come up.

Asked whether he had changed jobs or plans, he said no. Asked whether he knew
where to go when a specialist was needed, he said yes.

He is not the person this project was built for. He is included because he
shows what the advisor must not do: it must not walk someone through a problem
they solved years ago. A confident user needs the answer, not a lesson.

What the advisor should do for Dad: answer in one line, name the hospitals,
stop.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROFILE 2 — MUM: the user who changed plans
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mum changed insurance plans. Her card shows a class — Class A or Class B —
but when the new plan started she had no way of knowing which hospitals it
actually covered. She did not know where to go for a specialist, and nobody
had ever asked her to explain which hospital takes her insurance, so she had
never had to put the answer into words.

Her method is to find out after the fact, in person. Asked how she discovers
which hospitals take her new plan, she said:

    "I would have to find out through the category of my insurance and then
    I will go to the hospital and ask if it covers it or no."

Asked whether there is a single place to look this up, she said there is not:

    "No, I don't have a specific place to ask to know which tier covers which
    hospitals, but maybe each insurance company and their website we could
    know which tier covers which hospital, but not all insurance companies."

Both answers point at the same gap. The information she needs — which
hospitals accept her class — exists, but it is published inconsistently.
Some insurers publish it, some do not, and there is no one place to compare.

She is the user the project was built for. Her problem is not that the
information does not exist. It is that she has to travel to find out, and
she travels whether or not she is covered.

What the advisor should do for Mum: ask which insurer and which class, check
the published table, name the hospitals that match, and say plainly when the
insurer publishes nothing so she does not make a wasted trip.

What we already know: she takes medicine every day or sees the same doctor

The question to answer here: if her insurance changed, would she know whether

This is the highest-stakes profile. For Mum, a wrong answer costs a wasted
trip. Here, a wrong answer interrupts ongoing treatment.


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY THESE THREE, AND NOT AGE OR JOB
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The three differ by the state of their insurance, not by who they are. Age,
nationality and occupation do not change which hospitals a plan covers. What
changes the answer is whether the plan just changed, whether it is settled, and
whether the person depends on it for ongoing treatment.

That is why the advisor asks about insurer, class and city rather than about
the user. Those three facts are what determine the honest answer. Asking
anything else would collect information that does not change the response.
